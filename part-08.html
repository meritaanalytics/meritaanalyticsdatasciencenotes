#8.1 What statistics is, and types of data

Statistics is the science of learning from data when you are uncertain. You rarely see every customer, farm or transaction. You see a sample, and you want to say something about the whole population. Statistics gives you tools for two jobs: describing the data you have (descriptive statistics) and judging what it says about the wider world (inferential statistics).

The right tool depends on the type of each column. Data from the Palmer Station penguin study (CC0 licence, file penguins.csv, unchanged) has all the main types.

Type Meaning Example column Sensible summary
Nominal categories with no order species, island, sex counts, proportions, mode
Ordinal categories with an order small / medium / large median, counts per level
Discrete counted whole numbers eggs in a nest mean, counts
Continuous measured on a scale body_mass_g, flipper_length_mm mean, median, spread

Species, island and sex are nominal. The four measurements are continuous. Some measurements are missing, so most summaries below drop missing values first.

Common mistake. Do not average category codes. If sex were stored as 0 and 1, a mean of 0.5 would be a proportion, not a typical value. Always ask what a column means before you summarise it.

#8.2 Describing data: centre, spread and shape

A measure of centre says what is typical: the mean (balance point), the median (middle value) and the mode (most frequent). A measure of spread says how much values vary: the standard deviation (typical distance from the mean), the range, and the IQR (width of the middle 50 percent). Percentiles say what share of values fall below a point.

m = pen["body_mass_g"].dropna()
print("n:", len(m), "| mean:", round(m.mean(), 1), "| median:", m.median())
print("std:", round(m.std(), 1), "| min:", m.min(), "| max:", m.max())
q1, q3 = m.quantile([0.25, 0.75])
print("Q1:", q1, "| Q3:", q3, "| IQR:", q3 - q1)
print("90th percentile:", m.quantile(0.9))
print("most common value:", m.mode().iloc[0])

n: 342 | mean: 4201.8 | median: 4050.0
std: 802.0 | min: 2700.0 | max: 6300.0
Q1: 3550.0 | Q3: 4750.0 | IQR: 1200.0
90th percentile: 5400.0
most common value: 3800.0

Pandas uses the sample standard deviation (divides by n - 1), which is what you want when your data is a sample. np.std divides by n by default, so the two differ slightly.

Skewness measures lopsidedness. A right-skewed column has a long tail of large values, and the mean sits above the median. Annual consumer price inflation for Kenya (World Bank, series FP.CPI.TOTL.ZG, ODC-PDDL licence, filtered to Kenya) is a good example.

Practice

A matatu route has fares (Ksh) of 50, 50, 60, 70, 500. Which is a better 'typical fare', mean or median?

fares = pd.Series([50, 50, 60, 70, 500])
print("mean:", fares.mean(), "| median:", fares.median())

mean: 146.0 | median: 60.0

Solution. The single Ksh 500 fare drags the mean up to 146, while the median of 60 describes most trips. Use the median here.

#8.3 Probability and common distributions

Probability is a number from 0 to 1 that says how likely an event is. You can estimate it as a long-run relative frequency: how often the event happens out of many tries. Conditional probability, written P(A given B), is the chance of A once you know B happened. Two events are independent if knowing one tells you nothing about the other.

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
for n in [10, 1000, 100000]:
    print(f"{n:>7} fair coin flips -> share of heads: {rng.integers(0, 2, n).mean():.3f}")
tab = pd.crosstab(pen["island"], pen["species"])
print(tab)
print("P(Adelie):", round((pen["species"] == "Adelie").mean(), 3))
print("P(Adelie | Biscoe):", round(tab.loc["Biscoe", "Adelie"] / tab.loc["Biscoe"].sum(), 3))

   1000 fair coin flips -> share of heads: 0.514

The normal distribution is described by its mean and standard deviation. A z-score says how many standard deviations a value is from the mean: z = (x - mean) / std. About 68 percent of normal data lies within 1 standard deviation of the mean, 95 percent within 2, and 99.7 percent within 3.

print("actual share lighter:", round((ad < 3900).mean(), 3))
print("within 2 std:", round(stats.norm.cdf(2) - stats.norm.cdf(-2), 3))

Common mistake. pmf is for counts (probability of exactly k). pdf is for continuous values (a density, not a probability). Use cdf to get probabilities of ranges.

#8.4 Sampling, bias and the central limit theorem

A good sample looks like the population. A random sample gives every member an equal chance and is unbiased. Sampling bias appears when some members are more likely to be picked, for example surveying only people who answer an app notification. Bias does not shrink when you collect more data.

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
pop = pen["body_mass_g"].dropna().to_numpy()
random_s = rng.choice(pop, 40, replace=False)
biased_s = np.sort(pop)[-100:][rng.choice(100, 40, replace=False)]   # only the heaviest birds
print("population mean:", round(pop.mean(), 1))
print("random sample mean:", round(random_s.mean(), 1))
print("biased sample mean (heaviest only):", round(biased_s.mean(), 1))

population mean: 4201.8
random sample mean: 4295.6
biased sample mean (heaviest only): 5237.5

Different random samples give different means. The sampling distribution is the spread of a statistic (here the mean) over many repeated samples. Its standard deviation is the standard error (SE): SE = std / sqrt(n). A bigger sample gives a smaller SE, so a more precise estimate.

The central limit theorem says that the sample mean is close to normally distributed once n is moderately large, even when the data itself is skewed. We test it on the skewed inflation values of every country and year (World Bank; values above 50 percent dropped to remove hyperinflation outliers).

allinf = infl["Inflation"].dropna()

#8.5 Confidence intervals

A sample mean is a single guess. A confidence interval (CI) gives a range of plausible values for the true mean: estimate +/- critical value x SE. A 95 percent CI is built so that 95 percent of intervals made this way would contain the true value. It does not mean there is a 95 percent chance the true value is in this one interval.

x = pen[pen["species"] == "Gentoo"]["body_mass_g"].dropna()
n, mean, se = len(x), x.mean(), x.std() / np.sqrt(len(x))
tcrit = stats.t.ppf(0.975, df=n - 1)
print("n:", n, "| mean:", round(mean, 1), "| SE:", round(se, 1), "| t critical:", round(tcrit, 3))
print("95% CI by hand:", round(mean - tcrit * se, 1), "to", round(mean + tcrit * se, 1))
print("95% CI scipy  :", np.round(stats.t.interval(0.95, n - 1, loc=mean, scale=se), 1))

n: 123 | mean: 5076.0 | SE: 45.5 | t critical: 1.98
95% CI by hand: 4986.0 to 5166.0
95% CI scipy  : [4986. 5166.]

We use the t distribution instead of the normal because we estimated the standard deviation from the sample. It has slightly fatter tails, which matters most for small n. Now check what '95 percent' means by simulation: draw many samples, build an interval each time, and count how often it captures the true mean.

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
true_mean = pop.mean()
hit = 0
for _ in range(2000):
    s = rng.choice(pop, 30)
    lo, hi = stats.t.interval(0.95, 29, loc=s.mean(), scale=stats.sem(s))
    hit += lo <= true_mean <= hi
print("share of intervals containing the true mean:", hit / 2000)

share of intervals containing the true mean: 0.9545

Close to 0.95, as promised. Wider intervals come from more spread, a smaller sample or a higher confidence level (99 percent is wider than 95 percent).

Good habit. Reporting an estimate without any interval hides how uncertain it is. Say 'about 5,076 g (95 percent CI 4,986 to 5,166)', not just 'about 5,076 g'.

#8.6 The logic of hypothesis testing

A hypothesis test asks whether a pattern in a sample could just be chance. The steps are always the same.

  • State the null hypothesis (H0): there is no effect or no difference. State the alternative (H1): there is.
  • Choose a significance level alpha before looking, usually 0.05.
  • Compute a test statistic and its p-value: the probability of seeing a result at least this extreme if H0 were true.
  • If p is below alpha, reject H0; otherwise fail to reject it (this is not proof that H0 is true).

Two mistakes are possible. A Type I error is a false alarm: rejecting H0 when it is true; its rate is alpha. A Type II error is a miss: failing to reject H0 when there really is an effect. Power = 1 - P(Type II) is the chance of detecting a real effect. Power grows with sample size and effect size.

H0 is true H0 is false
Reject H0 Type I error (false alarm) Correct (power)
Do not reject H0 Correct Type II error (miss) 
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
def rate(diff, n=30, reps=3000):
    p = np.array([stats.ttest_ind(rng.normal(0, 1, n), rng.normal(diff, 1, n)).pvalue for _ in range(reps)])
    return p
p_null, p_real = rate(0.0), rate(0.8)
print("No true difference: share with p < 0.05 (Type I rate):", round((p_null < 0.05).mean(), 3))
print("True difference of 0.8 std: share with p < 0.05 (power):", round((p_real < 0.05).mean(), 3))

No true difference: share with p < 0.05 (Type I rate): 0.045
True difference of 0.8 std: share with p < 0.05 (power): 0.869
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 3.4), sharey=True)
ax[0].hist(p_null, bins=20, color=GREY); ax[0].set_title("No real effect: p-values are uniform")
ax[1].hist(p_real, bins=20, color=BLUE); ax[1].set_title("Real effect: p-values pile up near 0")
for a in ax: a.set_xlabel("p-value"); a.axvline(0.05, color=AMBER)
ax[0].set_ylabel("Simulated experiments")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.savefig("p08_pvals.png", dpi=130); plt.close(); print("saved p08_pvals.png")

saved p08_pvals.png

When nothing is going on, about 5 percent of tests still come out 'significant' (amber line): that is alpha at work. With a real effect, most p-values are small. You can also plan the sample size needed for a given power.

from statsmodels.stats.power import TTestIndPower
need = TTestIndPower().solve_power(effect_size=0.5, alpha=0.05, power=0.8)
print("per-group n to detect a medium effect (d=0.5) with 80% power:", int(np.ceil(need)))

per-group n to detect a medium effect (d=0.5) with 80% power: 64

Common mistake. A p-value is NOT the probability that H0 is true, and NOT the size of an effect. It only says how surprising the data would be if H0 were true.

#8.7 Common tests: t-tests, proportions, chi-square, ANOVA and rank tests

Every test in this section follows the four steps of Section 8.6. What changes is the question and the kind of data. All use scipy.stats. The penguin data (rows with missing values dropped) is our main example.

#One-sample t-test: is the mean different from a given value?

import os, numpy as np, pandas as pd
from pathlib import Path
from scipy import stats
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt, seaborn as sns
PUB = Path(os.environ["GUIDE_ROOT"]) / "datasets" / "public"
def load_public(name, url=None):
    p = PUB / name
    return pd.read_csv(p if p.exists() else url)
BLUE, GREEN, GREY, DARK, AMBER = "#1F5FD6", "#2BB38A", "#9CA3AF", "#0B2A4A", "#B45309"
sns.set_theme(style="whitegrid")
pen = load_public("penguins.csv").dropna()

adelie = pen[pen["species"] == "Adelie"]
t, p = stats.ttest_1samp(adelie["body_mass_g"], popmean=3700)
print("Adelie mean mass:", round(adelie["body_mass_g"].mean(), 1), "| t:", round(t, 2), "| p:", round(p, 4))

Adelie mean mass: 3706.2 | t: 0.16 | p: 0.8712

H0: the true mean is 3,700 g (an arbitrary reference value). The sample mean is almost exactly 3,700 g and p is large, so we fail to reject H0: the data is consistent with 3,700 g.

#Two-sample t-test (Welch): do two groups differ?

Welch's t-test compares the means of two independent groups and does not assume equal spread, so it is the safe default (equal_var=False).

f = adelie[adelie["sex"] == "FEMALE"]["body_mass_g"]
mm = adelie[adelie["sex"] == "MALE"]["body_mass_g"]
t, p = stats.ttest_ind(mm, f, equal_var=False)
print("male mean:", round(mm.mean()), "| female mean:", round(f.mean()), "| t:", round(t, 2), "| p:", p)

male mean: 4043 | female mean: 3369 | t: 13.13 | p: 6.402319748031804e-26

#Paired t-test: the same unit measured twice

When each row is measured twice (before and after, or two years for the same country), test the differences. Here: inflation in 2021 versus 2022 for the same countries (World Bank data, rows with both years).

inf = load_public("inflation_consumer.csv")
w = inf[inf["Year"].isin([2021, 2022])].pivot_table(index="Country", columns="Year", values="Inflation")
w = w.dropna()
w = w[(w < 60).all(axis=1)]
t, p = stats.ttest_rel(w[2022], w[2021])
print("countries:", len(w), "| mean 2021:", round(w[2021].mean(), 2), "| mean 2022:", round(w[2022].mean(), 2))
print("paired t:", round(t, 2), "| p:", p)

countries: 245 | mean 2021: 6.88 | mean 2022: 9.16
paired t: 5.27 | p: 3.0724500822227304e-07

Between 2021 and 2022 the average inflation rose and the p-value is tiny, so the rise is unlikely to be chance. (Countries share global shocks, so treat this as a teaching example, not a full economic analysis.)

#Proportion z-test and chi-square test

For yes/no outcomes, compare a proportion with a target using a z-test. For two categorical columns, the chi-square test of independence asks whether they are related by comparing observed counts with the counts expected if they were independent.

from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportions_ztest
males = (adelie["sex"] == "MALE").sum()
z, p = proportions_ztest(males, len(adelie), value=0.5)
print("male share among Adelie:", round(males / len(adelie), 3), "| z:", round(z, 2), "| p:", round(p, 3))
tab = pd.crosstab(pen["species"], pen["island"])
chi2, p, dof, expected = stats.chi2_contingency(tab)
print(tab)
print("chi2:", round(chi2, 1), "| dof:", dof, "| p:", p)

male share among Adelie: 0.5 | z: 0.0 | p: 1.0
island     Biscoe  Dream  Torgersen
species                            
Adelie         44     55         47
Chinstrap       0     68          0
Gentoo        119      0          0
chi2: 284.6 | dof: 4 | p: 2.2818915409873682e-60

The male share is exactly one half and p is 1, so there is no evidence of an unbalanced sex ratio. Species and island are strongly related: the p-value is essentially zero. Chi-square needs counts, not percentages.

#One-way ANOVA: three or more group means

Running many t-tests inflates false alarms (Section 8.11). One-way ANOVA tests all group means at once. H0: all group means are equal. A small p says at least one group differs; Tukey's test shows which.

groups = [g["body_mass_g"].to_numpy() for _, g in pen.groupby("species")]
F, p = stats.f_oneway(*groups)
print("F:", round(F, 1), "| p:", p)
from statsmodels.stats.multicomp import pairwise_tukeyhsd
print(pairwise_tukeyhsd(pen["body_mass_g"], pen["species"]).summary())

#Non-parametric (rank) tests

Non-parametric tests work on ranks, so they do not need normal data and are safer for skewed values or small samples. Mann-Whitney U replaces the two-sample t-test, Wilcoxon signed-rank replaces the paired t-test, and Kruskal-Wallis replaces one-way ANOVA. We use the disease-progression score from the scikit-learn diabetes data (442 patients, BSD licence), which is skewed.

from sklearn.datasets import load_diabetes
db = load_diabetes(as_frame=True).frame
db["bmi_group"] = pd.qcut(db["bmi"], 4, labels=["Q1", "Q2", "Q3", "Q4"])
lo, hi = db[db["bmi_group"] == "Q1"]["target"], db[db["bmi_group"] == "Q4"]["target"]
print("skew of target:", round(db["target"].skew(), 2))
print("Mann-Whitney Q1 vs Q4: p =", stats.mannwhitneyu(lo, hi).pvalue)
print("Wilcoxon 2022 vs 2021 inflation: p =", stats.wilcoxon(w[2022], w[2021]).pvalue)
parts = [g["target"] for _, g in db.groupby("bmi_group", observed=True)]

Common mistake. A p-value only answers 'is there an effect?'. It does not say whether the effect is big enough to matter. That comes next. Also, a non-significant result does not prove there is no difference.

#8.8 Effect size, correlation and causation

With enough data, even a tiny difference gives a small p-value. Effect size measures how big the difference is. Cohen's d is the difference in means divided by the pooled standard deviation. Rough guide: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large.

def cohens_d(a, b):
    sp = np.sqrt(((len(a) - 1) * a.var(ddof=1) + (len(b) - 1) * b.var(ddof=1)) / (len(a) + len(b) - 2))
    return (a.mean() - b.mean()) / sp
print("Adelie male vs female mass, d =", round(cohens_d(mm, f), 2))
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
a, b = rng.normal(100, 15, 200000), rng.normal(100.5, 15, 200000)   # synthetic: a tiny real gap
print("huge sample, tiny gap: p =", stats.ttest_ind(a, b).pvalue, "| d =", round(cohens_d(b, a), 3))

Adelie male vs female mass, d = 2.17
huge sample, tiny gap: p = 9.675237198299387e-29 | d = 0.035

The second pair is statistically significant but practically trivial: the groups differ by half a point on a scale where people vary by 15 (d is about 0.03). Always report the size and a confidence interval, not only the p-value.

Correlation measures how two numeric columns move together, from -1 to +1. Pearson's r measures straight-line relationships; Spearman's rho uses ranks, so it also captures steady curved relationships and resists outliers.

r, p = stats.pearsonr(pen["flipper_length_mm"], pen["body_mass_g"])
rho, _ = stats.spearmanr(pen["flipper_length_mm"], pen["body_mass_g"])
print("flipper vs mass: Pearson r =", round(r, 3), "| Spearman rho =", round(rho, 3))
x = np.arange(1, 11); y = np.exp(x / 2)          # steady but curved
print("curved but steady: Pearson", round(stats.pearsonr(x, y)[0], 3), "| Spearman", stats.spearmanr(x, y)[0])

flipper vs mass: Pearson r = 0.873 | Spearman rho = 0.84
curved but steady: Pearson 0.865 | Spearman 0.9999999999999999

Correlation is not causation. A third variable (a confounder) can create or even reverse a pattern. Across all penguins, longer bills go with shallower bills. Inside each species the link flips.

print("all penguins: r =", round(pen["bill_length_mm"].corr(pen["bill_depth_mm"]), 2))
print(pen.groupby("species")[["bill_length_mm", "bill_depth_mm"]].corr().xs("bill_length_mm", level=1)
      ["bill_depth_mm"].round(2).to_string())

#8.9 A/B testing end to end, with bootstrap and permutation tests

An A/B test is a randomised experiment: users are randomly assigned to version A (control) or B (new), and you compare an outcome. Randomisation removes confounders. Scenario (all data below is synthetic): a lending app tests a new 'Apply now' screen. The outcome is whether the user completes an application.

#Step 1: plan the sample size

from statsmodels.stats.power import NormalIndPower
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportion_effectsize
es = proportion_effectsize(0.12, 0.14)            # baseline 12 percent, hoping for 14 percent
n = NormalIndPower().solve_power(es, alpha=0.05, power=0.8)
print("users needed per group:", int(np.ceil(n)))

users needed per group: 4433

#Step 2: run the test and analyse

from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportions_ztest, confint_proportions_2indep
rng = np.random.default_rng(21)
nA = nB = 4500
convA, convB = rng.binomial(nA, 0.12), rng.binomial(nB, 0.14)     # synthetic outcomes
z, p = proportions_ztest([convB, convA], [nB, nA])
lo, hi = confint_proportions_2indep(convB, nB, convA, nA)
print(f"A: {convA/nA:.3%} | B: {convB/nB:.3%} | lift: {(convB/nB - convA/nA):.3%} points")
print(f"z = {z:.2f} | p = {p:.4f} | 95% CI of difference: {lo:.3%} to {hi:.3%}")

A: 11.689% | B: 12.956% | lift: 1.267% points
z = 1.83 | p = 0.0676 | 95% CI of difference: -0.092% to 2.626%

Here B converts a little better, but p is above 0.05 and the interval includes zero, so this test has not proven a lift. Decide with three things together: is p below alpha, is the interval away from zero, and is the lift big enough to be worth the cost of shipping? Do not stop the test early when the p-value first dips below 0.05: that 'peeking' raises the false alarm rate. Fix the sample size in advance.

#Bootstrap and permutation tests

Sometimes the usual formulas do not fit, for example for the mean of skewed revenue per user. Two simulation tricks need almost no assumptions. The bootstrap resamples your data with replacement many times to see how a statistic varies, which gives a confidence interval. A permutation test shuffles the group labels to see what differences arise by chance when labels mean nothing.

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
revA = rng.lognormal(3.0, 1.0, 400)        # synthetic revenue per user (Ksh), skewed
revB = rng.lognormal(3.15, 1.0, 400)
obs = revB.mean() - revA.mean()
boot = np.array([rng.choice(revB, 400).mean() - rng.choice(revA, 400).mean() for _ in range(5000)])
allv = np.concatenate([revA, revB])
perm = []
for _ in range(5000):
    rng.shuffle(allv)
    perm.append(allv[400:].mean() - allv[:400].mean())
perm = np.array(perm)
print("observed difference (Ksh):", round(obs, 1))
print("bootstrap 95% CI:", np.round(np.percentile(boot, [2.5, 97.5]), 1))
print("permutation p-value:", round((np.abs(perm) >= abs(obs)).mean(), 4))

observed difference (Ksh): 6.2
bootstrap 95% CI: [-0.5 13.4]
permutation p-value: 0.0778
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 3.6))
ax[0].hist(boot, bins=40, color=BLUE); ax[0].axvline(0, color=AMBER)
ax[0].set_title("Bootstrap: difference in mean revenue"); ax[0].set_xlabel("B minus A (Ksh)")
ax[0].set_ylabel("Resamples")
ax[1].hist(perm, bins=40, color=GREY); ax[1].axvline(obs, color=AMBER, label="observed")
ax[1].set_title("Permutation: differences by chance alone"); ax[1].set_xlabel("B minus A (Ksh)")
ax[1].set_ylabel("Shuffles"); ax[1].legend()
plt.tight_layout(); plt.savefig("p08_boot.png", dpi=130); plt.close(); print("saved p08_boot.png")

saved p08_boot.png

The bootstrap histogram shows plausible values of the true difference; the permutation histogram shows what chance alone produces. The observed value (amber) is compared with that chance distribution to give the p-value. Here the bootstrap interval includes zero and the permutation p is above 0.05, so the data does not show a clear revenue gain.

#8.10 (Advanced) Regression inference with statsmodels

Linear regression models an outcome as a straight-line function of one or more predictors. In statistics we care not only about predictions but about inference: how large is each coefficient, how uncertain is it (standard error), and could it be zero (p-value)? R-squared is the share of variation explained. Data: scikit-learn diabetes (442 patients; features are already centred and scaled; target is disease progression after one year). Model building for prediction is in Part 10.

import statsmodels.api as sm
from sklearn.datasets import load_diabetes
db = load_diabetes(as_frame=True).frame
X = sm.add_constant(db[["bmi"]])
m1 = sm.OLS(db["target"], X).fit()
print(m1.params.round(1).to_dict(), "| R2:", round(m1.rsquared, 3))
print("bmi: SE", round(m1.bse["bmi"], 1), "| p", m1.pvalues["bmi"])

{'const': 152.1, 'bmi': 949.4} | R2: 0.344
bmi: SE 62.5 | p 3.466006445167444e-42

The bmi coefficient says how much the progression score changes for a one-unit change in the scaled bmi, with the other variables not considered. Now add more predictors: blood pressure (bp) and a serum measure (s5).

The p-values are only trustworthy if the model's assumptions hold: a straight-line relationship, residuals (errors) with a constant spread and roughly normal shape, and independent observations. Check the residuals.

Residuals should look like a shapeless cloud around zero, and the Q-Q points should hug the line. Curves, funnels or heavy tails mean the standard errors may be off; consider a transformation or a different model.

Common mistake. A high R-squared does not mean the model is correct, and a tiny coefficient p-value does not mean the variable causes the outcome. Also watch for predictors that are strongly correlated with each other: their individual coefficients become unstable.

#8.11 (Advanced) Bayesian thinking and the multiple testing trap

Bayesian statistics treats the unknown quantity (such as a conversion rate) as having a probability distribution that you update as data arrives: prior belief + data = posterior belief. For a success rate the Beta distribution works neatly: start with Beta(a, b), observe s successes and f failures, and the posterior is Beta(a + s, b + f). Using the A/B counts from Section 8.9:

rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
postA = stats.beta(1 + convA, 1 + nA - convA)       # Beta(1,1) = flat prior
postB = stats.beta(1 + convB, 1 + nB - convB)
sa, sb = postA.rvs(100000, random_state=rng), postB.rvs(100000, random_state=rng)
print("posterior mean A:", round(postA.mean(), 4), "| B:", round(postB.mean(), 4))
print("95% credible interval for B:", np.round(postB.interval(0.95), 4))
print("probability B is truly better than A:", round((sb > sa).mean(), 3))

posterior mean A: 0.1171 | B: 0.1297
95% credible interval for B: [0.1201 0.1397]
probability B is truly better than A: 0.967

Where the z-test said 'not proven', the Bayesian view says B is probably better, but with real uncertainty about how much. The two views answer different questions. The last line is a direct, plain answer ('how likely is B better?') that a p-value cannot give. A credible interval is read the way people wrongly read a confidence interval: the parameter lies inside it with 95 percent probability, given the model and prior.

#The multiple testing trap

Each test at alpha = 0.05 has a 5 percent false alarm chance. Run 20 tests on pure noise and you expect about one 'discovery'. Corrections raise the bar. Bonferroni is simple and strict; Benjamini-Hochberg controls the share of false discoveries and is more powerful.

from statsmodels.stats.multitest import multipletests
rng = np.random.default_rng(99)
pvals = np.array([stats.ttest_ind(rng.normal(size=40), rng.normal(size=40)).pvalue for _ in range(40)])
print("tests on pure noise:", len(pvals), "| 'significant' at 0.05:", int((pvals < 0.05).sum()))
for method in ["bonferroni", "fdr_bh"]:
    print(method, "->", int(multipletests(pvals, alpha=0.05, method=method)[0].sum()), "significant")

tests on pure noise: 40 | 'significant' at 0.05: 5
bonferroni -> 0 significant
fdr_bh -> 0 significant

Common mistake. Testing many metrics, segments or variables and reporting only the 'significant' ones is called p-hacking. Decide your questions before you look, and correct for the number of tests you ran.

#8.12 Which test should I use?

Ask three questions: what is the outcome type, how many groups, and are the groups independent or paired? If the numeric data is very skewed or the sample small, use the rank-based alternative.

Question Data Standard test Rank-based alternative scipy function
One mean vs a value numeric one-sample t Wilcoxon signed-rank ttest_1samp
Two independent groups numeric Welch two-sample t Mann-Whitney U ttest_ind, mannwhitneyu
Same units twice numeric paired t Wilcoxon signed-rank ttest_rel, wilcoxon
Three or more groups numeric one-way ANOVA Kruskal-Wallis f_oneway, kruskal
One proportion vs a target yes/no proportion z-test exact binomial test proportions_ztest
Two proportions (A/B) yes/no two-proportion z-test Fisher exact proportions_ztest
Two categorical columns counts chi-square independence Fisher exact (small counts) chi2_contingency
Two numeric columns related numeric Pearson correlation Spearman pearsonr, spearmanr

Checklist. Before any test: look at the data (Part 5, Part 7), check for outliers and missing values, state H0 and alpha first, report the effect size with a confidence interval, and remember that statistics shows association unless the data came from a randomised experiment.

Key takeaways
  • Choose summaries by data type; for skewed data report the median beside the mean.
  • The standard error shrinks with sqrt(n); the central limit theorem makes sample means roughly normal.
  • A confidence interval shows the plausible range of the truth; a p-value only measures surprise under H0.
  • Report effect size and intervals, not just 'significant'; a tiny effect can be significant in big data.
  • Correlation is not causation: confounders can reverse a trend; randomised A/B tests remove them.
  • Fix sample size before an A/B test, do not peek, and correct for many tests (Bonferroni, BH).
