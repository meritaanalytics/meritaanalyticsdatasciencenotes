#17.1 How a Business Makes Money

Every business sells something for a price and pays costs to do it. Revenue is price times quantity sold. Variable costs rise with each sale (parts, airtime, delivery). Fixed costs do not (rent, licences, salaries). Profit is revenue minus all costs.

Contribution margin (CM) is price minus variable cost per unit: what one sale contributes toward the fixed costs. The CM ratio is CM divided by price. Break-even is the sales level where profit is exactly zero: fixed costs divided by the CM ratio. Our example is a synthetic TV repair shop in Nairobi, with made-up prices.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

jobs = pd.DataFrame({
    "service": ["Screen repair", "Board repair", "HDMI port fix", "Wall mounting"],
    "price_ksh": [9500, 4500, 2500, 1500],
    "variable_ksh": [7000, 2700, 1300, 650],      # parts plus labour per job
    "jobs_per_month": [14, 30, 22, 40],
})
jobs["cm_per_job"] = jobs["price_ksh"] - jobs["variable_ksh"]
jobs["cm_ratio_pct"] = (100 * jobs["cm_per_job"] / jobs["price_ksh"]).round(1)
jobs["total_cm"] = jobs["cm_per_job"] * jobs["jobs_per_month"]
print(jobs[["service", "cm_per_job", "cm_ratio_pct", "total_cm"]].to_string(index=False))

      service  cm_per_job  cm_ratio_pct  total_cm
Screen repair        2500          26.3     35000

HDMI port fix        1200          48.0     26400
Wall mounting         850          56.7     34000
fixed_costs = 95000                                   # rent, power, one assistant (illustrative)
revenue = (jobs["price_ksh"] * jobs["jobs_per_month"]).sum()
total_cm = jobs["total_cm"].sum()
cm_ratio = total_cm / revenue
be_revenue = fixed_costs / cm_ratio
print(f"Revenue Ksh {revenue:,.0f} | CM Ksh {total_cm:,.0f} | blended CM ratio {cm_ratio:.1%}")
print(f"Profit: Ksh {total_cm - fixed_costs:,.0f} | break-even revenue: Ksh {be_revenue:,.0f}")

Plug the answer back into the formula: profit at break-even must be zero. The result assumes the mix of jobs stays the same.

Common mistake. A high price does not mean a high margin. Compare services by CM ratio, not by price: here the cheapest service earns a better ratio than the most expensive one.

#17.2 Growth and Retention Metrics

Growth rate is (this period - last period) / last period. Churn is the share of customers lost in a period, and retention is 1 minus churn. A cohort is a group who started in the same month; a retention matrix shows whether newer groups stay longer than older ones, which one overall average would hide.

rev = pd.Series([410, 436, 452, 441, 470, 505], name="revenue_k",
                index=pd.period_range("2025-01", periods=6, freq="M"))
growth = (rev.pct_change() * 100).round(1)
cagr_like = ((rev.iloc[-1] / rev.iloc[0]) ** (1 / (len(rev) - 1)) - 1) * 100
print(pd.DataFrame({"revenue_k": rev, "mom_growth_pct": growth}).to_string())
print(f"Average compound monthly growth: {cagr_like:.2f}%")

#17.3 Acquisition Cost, Payback and Lifetime Value

Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is marketing spend divided by new customers won. Payback period is CAC divided by the monthly gross margin per customer: how many months until a customer has paid back what it cost to win them. A simple lifetime value (LTV) is monthly margin divided by monthly churn. The LTV to CAC ratio is a quick health check (many teams like 3 or more; that is a rule of thumb, not a law).

ch = pd.DataFrame({
    "channel": ["WhatsApp ads", "Radio", "Referral", "Roadside poster"],
    "spend_ksh": [60000, 90000, 48000, 15000],
    "new_customers": [150, 120, 80, 25],
    "monthly_margin_ksh": [350, 300, 350, 250],
    "monthly_churn": [0.08, 0.06, 0.06, 0.10],
})
ch["CAC"] = ch["spend_ksh"] / ch["new_customers"]
ch["payback_months"] = ch["CAC"] / ch["monthly_margin_ksh"]
ch["LTV"] = ch["monthly_margin_ksh"] / ch["monthly_churn"]
ch["LTV_to_CAC"] = ch["LTV"] / ch["CAC"]
print(ch[["channel", "CAC", "payback_months", "LTV", "LTV_to_CAC"]].round(1).to_string(index=False))
blended = ch["spend_ksh"].sum() / ch["new_customers"].sum()
print(f"Blended CAC: Ksh {blended:,.0f} | best LTV:CAC: {ch.loc[ch['LTV_to_CAC'].idxmax(), 'channel']}")

        channel   CAC  payback_months    LTV  LTV_to_CAC

          Radio 750.0             2.5 5000.0         6.7
       Referral 600.0             1.7 5833.3         9.7
Roadside poster 600.0             2.4 2500.0         4.2

Common mistake. Reporting only the blended CAC hides that one channel may cost several times another. Compare by channel, and use gross margin, not revenue, for payback.

#17.4 Cash Flow and Runway

Profit is not cash: a customer who has not paid yet is revenue but not cash. Operating cash flow comes from the business, investing is equipment, and financing is loans and repayments. Net cash flow is the sum of the three and the closing balance is the opening balance plus the running total. Runway is cash divided by monthly burn.

months = pd.period_range("2025-01", "2025-12", freq="M")
rng = np.random.default_rng(97)
cf = pd.DataFrame({
    "operating": rng.normal(60000, 25000, 12).round(-2),
    "investing": np.where(months.month == 4, -320000, 0),       # buy equipment in April
    "financing": np.where(months.month == 4, 100000, -12000),   # SACCO loan, then repayments
}, index=months.astype(str))
opening = 50000
cf["net"] = cf.sum(axis=1)
cf["closing"] = opening + cf["net"].cumsum()
print(cf.to_string())
print("lowest balance:", f"{cf['closing'].min():,.0f}", "in", cf["closing"].idxmin())
print("months below zero:", cf.index[cf["closing"] < 0].tolist())

#17.6 Credit and Lending Metrics

Lenders measure risk with a few numbers. PD (probability of default) is the chance a borrower defaults within a stated period. EAD (exposure at default) is the amount owed when that happens. LGD (loss given default) is the share of EAD not recovered. Expected loss is EL = PD x LGD x EAD. These are teaching definitions on synthetic data; regulated lenders follow detailed supervisory rules (such as IFRS 9).

rng = np.random.default_rng(907)
n = 4000
band = rng.choice(["A (low risk)", "B", "C", "D (high risk)"], n, p=[.30, .35, .25, .10])
pd_map = {"A (low risk)": .02, "B": .06, "C": .14, "D (high risk)": .30}   # illustrative
book = pd.DataFrame({
    "band": band,
    "ead_ksh": rng.lognormal(10.8, 0.6, n).round(-2),
    "default": rng.random(n) < pd.Series(band).map(pd_map).to_numpy(),
})
book["recovery"] = np.where(book["default"], rng.beta(3, 4, n), np.nan)
seg = book.groupby("band").agg(loans=("default", "size"), PD=("default", "mean"),
                               EAD=("ead_ksh", "sum"))
d = book[book["default"]]
lost = ((1 - d["recovery"]) * d["ead_ksh"]).groupby(d["band"]).sum()
seg["LGD"] = lost / d["ead_ksh"].groupby(d["band"]).sum()
seg["EL_ksh"] = seg["PD"] * seg["LGD"] * seg["EAD"]
print(seg.round({"PD": 3, "LGD": 3, "EL_ksh": 0}).to_string())
print(f"Portfolio EL: Ksh {seg['EL_ksh'].sum():,.0f} = {100 * seg['EL_ksh'].sum() / seg['EAD'].sum():.2f}% of EAD")

#NPL ratio and PAR30

The NPL ratio is non-performing loans divided by gross loans; a loan is commonly non-performing at 90 or more days past due (your regulator's definition decides). PAR30 counts balances more than 30 days past due. The synthetic lender database has no balance column, so principal stands in for the balance.

import sqlite3
from fintech_db import build_fintech_db
con = sqlite3.connect(build_fintech_db("p17_lender.db"))
loans = pd.read_sql("SELECT * FROM loans", con)
live = loans[loans["status"] != "Closed"]                     # not Closed = the live book
gross = live["principal_ksh"].sum()
npl = live.loc[live["days_past_due"] >= 90, "principal_ksh"].sum()
par30 = live.loc[live["days_past_due"] > 30, "principal_ksh"].sum()
print(f"Gross Ksh {gross:,.0f} | NPL ratio {npl / gross:.1%} | PAR30 {par30 / gross:.1%}")
print("PAR30 is at least the NPL ratio:", bool(par30 >= npl))

Gross Ksh 66,882,700 | NPL ratio 46.6% | PAR30 50.3%
PAR30 is at least the NPL ratio: True

The ratios are high because this synthetic book keeps defaulted loans at full principal with no write-offs. A real lender would report far less.

#Scorecard quality: AUC and KS

A scorecard ranks borrowers by risk. AUC is the chance that a randomly chosen defaulter scores worse than a randomly chosen non-defaulter: 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect. KS is the largest gap between the cumulative share of defaulters and non-defaulters as you move down the score. Both measure separation, not calibration. We build a small synthetic scorecard, then compute both and check against scikit-learn and SciPy.

from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score
from scipy.stats import ks_2samp

rng = np.random.default_rng(908)
n = 6000
risk = rng.normal(0, 1, n)                                    # hidden true risk
y = (rng.random(n) < 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(-2.3 + risk)))).astype(int)   # 1 = defaulted
score = np.round(620 - 55 * (risk + rng.normal(0, 0.8, n)))   # imperfect score, high = good

def ks_stat(y, s):
    t = pd.DataFrame({"y": y, "s": s}).groupby("s")["y"].agg(bad="sum", n="size").sort_index()
    bad = t["bad"].cumsum() / t["bad"].sum()
    good = (t["n"] - t["bad"]).cumsum() / (t["n"] - t["bad"]).sum()
    return (bad - good).abs().max()

auc, ks = roc_auc_score(y, -score), ks_stat(y, score)
print(f"default rate {y.mean():.3f} | AUC {auc:.3f} | Gini {2 * auc - 1:.3f} | KS {ks:.3f}")
print("KS matches SciPy:", bool(np.isclose(ks, ks_2samp(score[y == 1], score[y == 0]).statistic)))
bands = pd.cut(score, [0, 520, 570, 620, 670, 1000], right=False,
               labels=["<520", "520-569", "570-619", "620-669", "670+"])
tab = pd.DataFrame({"band": bands, "y": y}).groupby("band", observed=True)["y"].agg(["size", "mean"])
tab["mean"] = (100 * tab["mean"]).round(1)
print(tab.rename(columns={"size": "borrowers", "mean": "default_rate_pct"}).to_string())

default rate 0.125 | AUC 0.701 | Gini 0.403 | KS 0.308
KS matches SciPy: True

Using it in the real world. Measure AUC and KS on held-out data, and never use a feature only known after default (leakage). In practice monitor them monthly: a falling AUC means the score is ageing.

#17.7 Power BI: Power Query and the Star Schema

Power BI is Microsoft's dashboard tool. Power Query is its data-preparation engine (Get Data, then Transform Data). #The star schema

Power BI works best with a star schema: a central fact table of events (transactions) with numbers to add up, surrounded by small dimension tables that describe them (customer, channel, date). A dimension's unique key relates one-to-many to the fact table, and filters flow from the dimension to the fact. Build one from the lender database and run three tests: unique dimension keys, no orphan keys, same totals as the source.

txn = pd.read_sql("SELECT * FROM transactions", con)
cust = pd.read_sql("SELECT * FROM customers", con)
dim_customer = cust[["customer_id", "county", "occupation", "segment"]].copy()
dim_channel = pd.DataFrame({"channel": sorted(txn["channel"].unique())})
dim_channel["channel_key"] = range(1, len(dim_channel) + 1)
fact_txn = (txn.merge(dim_channel, on="channel", validate="many_to_one")
               .assign(date_key=lambda t: t["txn_date"].str.replace("-", "").astype(int))
               [["txn_id", "customer_id", "date_key", "channel_key", "status", "amount_ksh"]])
dates = pd.date_range("2024-01-01", "2025-12-31", freq="D")
dim_date = pd.DataFrame({"date": dates, "date_key": dates.strftime("%Y%m%d").astype(int),
                         "year": dates.year, "month": dates.month,
                         "year_month": dates.strftime("%Y-%m")})
tests = {
    "dim keys unique": dim_customer["customer_id"].is_unique and dim_date["date_key"].is_unique,
    "date table has no gaps": bool((dim_date["date"].diff().dropna() == pd.Timedelta(days=1)).all()),
    "no orphan customers": fact_txn["customer_id"].isin(dim_customer["customer_id"]).all(),
    "no orphan dates": fact_txn["date_key"].isin(dim_date["date_key"]).all(),
    "fact total = source total": bool(np.isclose(fact_txn["amount_ksh"].sum(), txn["amount_ksh"].sum())),
}
print(pd.Series(tests).astype(bool).to_string())
assert all(tests.values())

Run these tests every time the model is rebuilt. An orphan key shows up in Power BI as a blank row in every visual.

Rule. Keep relationships one-to-many with a single filter direction. Avoid many-to-many and two-way filters until you can explain why you need them. Mark the date table as the date table, and keep it free of gaps, or time-intelligence functions return blanks.

#17.8 DAX Measures with a pandas Proof

DAX (Data Analysis Expressions) is the formula language of Power BI. A measure is not stored in a column; it is recalculated for the filter context of every visual cell (the row, column, slicer and page filters active there). In pandas you get the same number by filtering first, then aggregating. The DAX below is for reading; each line has a pandas proof so you know what number it must return.

Total Value = SUM ( fact_txn[amount_ksh] )
Txn Count   = COUNTROWS ( fact_txn )
Success Rate =
    DIVIDE ( CALCULATE ( [Txn Count], fact_txn[status] = "Success" ), [Txn Count] )
M-Pesa Value = CALCULATE ( [Total Value], dim_channel[channel] = "M-Pesa" )
Share of All Counties =
    DIVIDE ( [Total Value], CALCULATE ( [Total Value], ALL ( dim_customer[county] ) ) )
Value LY = CALCULATE ( [Total Value], SAMEPERIODLASTYEAR ( dim_date[date] ) )
YoY %    = DIVIDE ( [Total Value] - [Value LY], [Value LY] )

#17.9 The SQL to Python to Power BI Workflow

The common pattern is: database, then Python for cleaning, validation and metrics, then a tidy dataset, then Power BI for visuals. The skill is knowing which tool owns which step, so every rule is written once and every report agrees.

Step Best home Why
Filter, join and aggregate big tables SQL query or view runs where the data lives
Shared definitions (active customer, net revenue) one SQL view or one DAX measure one definition for all reports
Regex cleaning, fuzzy matching, outlier rules Python expressive, testable, loggable
Validation and reconciliation Python reusable, schedulable
Renames, type changes Power Query stays inside the refresh
Ratios and time intelligence DAX measures must react to slicers
Visuals, slicers, row-level security Power BI what stakeholders use

#A runnable workflow

SQLite stands in for SQL Server so every cell runs anywhere. The five steps are extract with SQL, clean in pandas, validate, reconcile against an independent SQL total, and publish tidy files for Power BI. Small functions make a pipeline you can schedule later (Part 18).

import json

EXTRACT_SQL = '''
SELECT t.txn_id, t.customer_id, t.txn_date, t.channel, t.status, t.amount_ksh, c.county, c.segment
FROM transactions t JOIN customers c ON c.customer_id = t.customer_id
'''

def clean(raw):
    df = raw.copy()
    for col in ["channel", "status", "county", "segment"]:
        df[col] = df[col].str.strip()
    df["txn_date"] = pd.to_datetime(df["txn_date"], format="%Y-%m-%d", errors="coerce")
    df["amount_ksh"] = pd.to_numeric(df["amount_ksh"], errors="coerce")
    df["year_month"] = df["txn_date"].dt.strftime("%Y-%m")
    df["is_success"] = (df["status"] == "Success").astype(int)
    return df

def validate(df):
    return {
        "duplicate txn_id": int(df["txn_id"].duplicated().sum()),
        "bad date": int(df["txn_date"].isna().sum()),
        "bad amount": int((df["amount_ksh"].isna() | (df["amount_ksh"] <= 0)).sum()),
        "unknown status": int((~df["status"].isin(["Success", "Failed", "Reversed"])).sum()),
    }

raw = pd.read_sql(EXTRACT_SQL, con)
df = clean(raw)
broken = pd.concat([raw, raw.iloc[[0]], raw.iloc[[1]].assign(amount_ksh=-50)], ignore_index=True)
print(pd.DataFrame({"real data": validate(df), "broken on purpose": validate(clean(broken))}))

Test your validator on data you broke on purpose; a check that never fails proves nothing.

def reconcile(df, con):
    sql = pd.read_sql("SELECT strftime('%Y-%m', txn_date) AS year_month, COUNT(*) AS n, "
                      "ROUND(SUM(amount_ksh), 2) AS total FROM transactions GROUP BY 1", con)
    py = df.groupby("year_month").agg(n=("txn_id", "size"), total=("amount_ksh", "sum")).round(2).reset_index()
    m = sql.merge(py, on="year_month", suffixes=("_sql", "_py"), validate="one_to_one")
    return bool((m["n_sql"] == m["n_py"]).all() and np.allclose(m["total_sql"], m["total_py"]))

def publish(df, out_dir):
    os.makedirs(out_dir, exist_ok=True)
    kpi = (df.groupby("year_month")
             .agg(txns=("txn_id", "size"), value_ksh=("amount_ksh", "sum"),
                  success_rate=("is_success", "mean"), active_customers=("customer_id", "nunique"))
             .round({"value_ksh": 2, "success_rate": 4}).reset_index())
    df.assign(txn_date=df["txn_date"].dt.strftime("%Y-%m-%d")).to_csv(f"{out_dir}/fact_transactions.csv", index=False)
    kpi.to_csv(f"{out_dir}/kpi_monthly.csv", index=False)
    return kpi

issues = validate(df)

#17.10 One-Page Dashboard Design Checklist

A dashboard is judged in ten seconds. Before you share one, tick every line.

Area Check
Purpose One audience and one decision it supports; the title says what it measures
Top row 3 to 5 KPI cards with definition, period and comparison (last month or target)
Layout Most important at top left; one idea per visual; no more than about 6 visuals
Chart choice Line for trend, bar for comparison, table for exact values; no pie with many slices
Colour One accent colour for the thing to notice; grey for the rest; readable without colour
Labels Units (Ksh, %), axis titles and a readable number format (Ksh 1.2M)
Filters A few slicers (date, county, product); default to a sensible period
Trust Reconciled to an independent SQL total; refresh date and data source shown
Access Read-only data account; row-level security if people should see only their region

Before you share. Reconcile three numbers (a row count, one total, one ratio) against an independent SQL query, write each KPI definition on the page, and show the refresh date. These habits prevent most arguments about whose number is right.

Key takeaways
  • Margin, not price, tells you which sales help: contribution margin pays the fixed costs, and break-even is fixed costs divided by the CM ratio.
  • Compare churn, CAC and payback by channel or cohort; blended averages hide the story.
  • Profit is not cash: track the closing balance and runway.
  • Every Excel formula has a pandas twin; check the pivot's grand total against the raw column sum.
  • Expected loss = PD x LGD x EAD; NPL and PAR30 describe the book, and AUC and KS describe a scorecard on held-out data.
  • Power BI wants a star schema, a gap-free date table and measures; prove each measure in pandas and reconcile before sharing.
