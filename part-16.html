Generative AI and Large Language Models

How text generators work, from a tiny word-counting model to attention, plus safe, checked use of AI at work.

In this part

  • Build a tiny text generator and see what a language model really does
  • Turn words into vectors and compare them with cosine similarity
  • Control a model with sampling settings and prompting techniques
  • Build a small retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline and check AI output safely

#16.1 Discriminative versus generative models

Most models in Parts 10 to 13 are discriminative: given inputs, they predict a label or a number (will this loan default? what is the price?). A generative model learns what the data itself looks like and can produce new examples: a sentence, an image, a table row. Chat assistants are generative models for text.

Discriminative Generative
Question answered Given x, what is y? What do examples like x look like?
Output A label or number New text, images, audio, data
Example Credit default classifier A chat assistant, an image generator
Judged by Accuracy, AUC, error Usefulness, correctness, safety (harder)

A large language model (LLM) is a generative model trained on huge amounts of text to predict the next piece of text. Everything else, including answering questions, follows from doing that one job very well.

#16.2 Language modelling and tokens

Models do not read letters or words directly. Text is cut into tokens: whole words, parts of words, or single characters. The model reads tokens and each token is a number in a fixed list (the vocabulary). Common words are one token; rare words split into pieces.

text = "mobile money agents helped customers withdraw cash"
print("word tokens:", text.split())
vocab = ["mobile", "money", "agent", "s", "help", "ed", "customer", "withdraw", "cash"]
def subword(word):                       # greedy longest-match, a toy version of real tokenizers
    out = []
    while word:
        for k in range(len(word), 0, -1):
            if word[:k] in vocab: out.append(word[:k]); word = word[k:]; break
        else: out.append(word[0]); word = word[1:]
    return out
print("subword tokens:", [t for w in text.split() for t in subword(w)])

word tokens: ['mobile', 'money', 'agents', 'helped', 'customers', 'withdraw', 'cash']
subword tokens: ['mobile', 'money', 'agent', 's', 'help', 'ed', 'customer', 's', 'withdraw', 'cash']

A language model gives the probability of the next token given the previous ones. The smallest version just counts: after the words 'money', how often does each word follow? That is an n-gram model. Below we count word pairs in a short text we wrote ourselves, then generate new text by sampling.

import numpy as np
from collections import defaultdict
corpus = ("the shop sells maize flour and the shop sells rice and beans . "
          "the farmer sells maize and the farmer sells beans at the market . "
          "the rider carries rice to the shop and the rider carries flour to the market . "
          "the market sells rice and maize flour every morning .")
words = corpus.split()
follow = defaultdict(list)
for a, b in zip(words, words[1:]): follow[a].append(b)
print("words seen after 'sells':", sorted(set(follow["sells"])))
print("count of each after 'the':", {w: follow["the"].count(w) for w in sorted(set(follow["the"]))})

words seen after 'sells': ['beans', 'maize', 'rice']
count of each after 'the': {'farmer': 2, 'market': 3, 'rider': 2, 'shop': 3}
def generate(start, n=14, seed=3):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed); out = [start]
    for _ in range(n):
        out.append(str(rng.choice(follow[out[-1]])))     # sample next word by observed frequency
    return " ".join(out)
for s in [3, 4, 5]: print(generate("the", seed=s))

the market . the farmer sells rice and the shop sells rice to the market
the rider carries flour to the market sells maize and the farmer sells beans at
the shop and the market . the shop sells rice and beans . the market

The sentences look plausible but drift and repeat, because each word only sees one word of history. Real LLMs use thousands of previous tokens and billions of learned numbers instead of a count table, but the loop is the same: predict a distribution over the next token, pick one, append, repeat.

#16.3 Embeddings: words as vectors

A computer needs numbers. An embedding gives each word a short list of numbers (a vector) so that words used in similar ways get similar vectors. A simple way to build them: count which words appear near each other, then compress the count table with SVD (a matrix factorisation from Part 13's dimension reduction).

import numpy as np
sents = ["farmer grows maize", "farmer grows beans", "farmer sells maize", "trader sells beans",
         "trader sells rice", "farmer grows rice", "technician repairs phone", "technician repairs screen",
         "technician fixes phone", "technician fixes screen", "customer buys phone", "customer buys screen",
         "customer buys maize", "trader buys rice"]
vocab = sorted({w for s in sents for w in s.split()}); ix = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
C = np.zeros((len(vocab), len(vocab)))
for s in sents:
    t = s.split()
    for i in range(len(t)):
        for j in range(len(t)):
            if i != j: C[ix[t[i]], ix[t[j]]] += 1          # co-occurs in the same short sentence
tot = C.sum(); pw = C.sum(1, keepdims=True) / tot; pc = C.sum(0, keepdims=True) / tot
ppmi = np.maximum(np.log((C / tot + 1e-12) / (pw * pc)), 0)    # positive pointwise mutual information
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(ppmi)
E = U[:, :3] * S[:3]                                           # 3-number embedding per word
print("vocabulary size:", len(vocab), "| embedding shape:", E.shape)

vocabulary size: 14 | embedding shape: (14, 3)

Cosine similarity compares the direction of two vectors: 1 means the same direction, 0 unrelated, -1 opposite. It ignores length, which is why it suits embeddings.

def cos(a, b): return float(a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b)))
def sim(w1, w2): return round(cos(E[ix[w1]], E[ix[w2]]), 2)
for pair in [("maize", "beans"), ("maize", "rice"), ("phone", "screen"), ("maize", "phone")]:
    print(f"{pair[0]:>6} vs {pair[1]:<6} cosine = {sim(*pair)}")

 maize vs beans  cosine = 0.97
 maize vs rice   cosine = 0.98
 phone vs screen cosine = 1.0
 maize vs phone  cosine = 0.37

Food words should score closer to each other than to phone words. The model was never told what a phone is; it inferred it from where the word appears. Modern LLMs learn embeddings with hundreds or thousands of numbers per token, and also embed whole sentences for search (used in 16.7).

#16.4 How LLMs are trained

An LLM is built in stages. Each stage changes what the model is good at.

Stage What happens Plain picture
Pretraining Predict the next token over a huge text collection Reading a vast library
Fine-tuning Continue training on a smaller, focused set Studying a specialty, e.g. banking
Instruction tuning Train on (instruction, good answer) pairs Learning to follow requests
RLHF People rank answers; a reward model learns taste; RL tunes the model A coach rewarding better answers

Pretraining gives broad knowledge and fluent language but not manners: a pretrained model may just continue your sentence. Instruction tuning makes it behave like an assistant. RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback, linked to Part 15) pushes it toward answers people judge helpful and safe. None of these steps guarantees truth: the model learns to produce likely text, not verified facts.

Remember. Training data has a cut-off date. An LLM does not know about events after it, unless you give it the information in the prompt (see RAG in 16.7).

#16.5 Sampling: temperature, top-k and top-p

At each step the model produces a score for every token in the vocabulary. These scores become probabilities (softmax, Part 14). Sampling decides how to pick from them. Temperature divides the scores first: low temperature sharpens the distribution (safe, repetitive), high temperature flattens it (varied, riskier). Top-k keeps only the k most likely tokens. Top-p (nucleus) keeps the smallest set whose probabilities add up to p.

import numpy as np
tokens = ["loan", "payment", "account", "banana", "the", "zebra"]
logits = np.array([3.0, 2.5, 2.0, 0.5, 1.5, -1.0])            # made-up scores for the next word
def probs(temp=1.0):
    z = logits / temp; z = z - z.max(); e = np.exp(z); return e / e.sum()
for T in [0.5, 1.0, 2.0]:
    print(f"T={T}:", dict(zip(tokens, probs(T).round(2).tolist())))

T=0.5: {'loan': 0.64, 'payment': 0.24, 'account': 0.09, 'banana': 0.0, 'the': 0.03, 'zebra': 0.0}
T=1.0: {'loan': 0.44, 'payment': 0.26, 'account': 0.16, 'banana': 0.04, 'the': 0.1, 'zebra': 0.01}
T=2.0: {'loan': 0.3, 'payment': 0.24, 'account': 0.18, 'banana': 0.09, 'the': 0.14, 'zebra': 0.04}
def top_k(p, k):
    q = p.copy(); q[np.argsort(p)[:-k]] = 0; return q / q.sum()
def top_p(p, thr):
    order = np.argsort(-p); keep = np.cumsum(p[order]) - p[order] < thr
    q = np.zeros_like(p); q[order[keep]] = p[order[keep]]; return q / q.sum()
p = probs(1.0); rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
print("top-k=2 :", dict(zip(tokens, top_k(p, 2).round(2).tolist())))
print("top-p=.9:", dict(zip(tokens, top_p(p, 0.9).round(2).tolist())))
for name, q in [("T=0.5", probs(0.5)), ("T=2.0", probs(2.0)), ("top-p .9", top_p(p, 0.9))]:
    print(f"{name:>8} ->", rng.choice(tokens, 10, p=q).tolist())

top-k=2 : {'loan': 0.62, 'payment': 0.38, 'account': 0.0, 'banana': 0.0, 'the': 0.0, 'zebra': 0.0}
top-p=.9: {'loan': 0.46, 'payment': 0.28, 'account': 0.17, 'banana': 0.0, 'the': 0.1, 'zebra': 0.0}
   T=0.5 -> ['payment', 'payment', 'loan', 'loan', 'loan', 'loan', 'loan', 'loan', 'loan', 'the']
   T=2.0 -> ['account', 'loan', 'payment', 'zebra', 'the', 'the', 'payment', 'payment', 'account', 'loan']
top-p .9 -> ['payment', 'loan', 'account', 'loan', 'payment', 'account', 'loan', 'account', 'account', 'loan']

BLUE, GREEN, GREY, DARK, AMBER = "#1F5FD6", "#2BB38A", "#9CA3AF", "#0B2A4A", "#B45309"

Practical rule: use low temperature for facts, extraction and code; higher temperature for brainstorming names or wording.

#16.6 Prompting techniques

A prompt is the text you give the model. Clear prompts get better answers. The main techniques:

Technique What you do Use when
Zero-shot Ask directly, no examples Simple, common tasks
Few-shot Show 2 to 4 input/output examples first You need a specific format or label style
Role / context Say who it is helping and for what Tone and detail matter
Structured output Ask for JSON or a fixed table Another program reads the answer
Step by step Ask it to reason before answering Maths, logic, multi-step checks 
examples = [("Send Ksh 500 to Mary", "Send Money"), ("Pay KPLC token Ksh 1000", "Bill Payment"),
            ("Buy Ksh 50 airtime", "Airtime")]
def few_shot(new_text):
    shots = "\n".join(f"Message: {m}\nCategory: {c}" for m, c in examples)
    return ("You label M-Pesa style messages. Reply with JSON only: {\"category\": \"...\"}.\n\n"
            f"{shots}\nMessage: {new_text}\nCategory:")
print(few_shot("Withdraw Ksh 2000 at the agent"))

When a program reads the reply, always validate it. Models sometimes return text that is almost, but not quite, valid JSON.

import json
replies = ['{"category": "Withdrawal"}', 'Sure! The category is Withdrawal.']
allowed = {"Send Money", "Withdrawal", "Deposit", "Bill Payment", "Airtime"}
for r in replies:
    try:
        cat = json.loads(r)["category"]
        print("ok     :", cat, "| allowed:", cat in allowed)
    except (json.JSONDecodeError, KeyError):
        print("reject :", r[:30], "-> ask again or flag for review")

ok     : Withdrawal | allowed: True
reject : Sure! The category is Withdraw -> ask again or flag for review

Tip. Common mistake: a vague prompt ('analyse this'). Say the goal, the data columns, the format you want, and what to do when unsure (for example 'say I do not know').

#16.7 Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

An LLM only knows what it was trained on. Retrieval-augmented generation fixes this: first RETRIEVE the most relevant passages from your own documents, then paste them into the prompt and tell the model to answer only from them. This gives current, company-specific answers and lets you cite the source. Retrieval can use embeddings; here we use TF-IDF (Part 11), which scores words that are frequent in a document but rare overall. The documents below are invented for this demo.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
docs = {
 "D1 Savings": "Members must save at least Ksh 500 each month. Savings are withdrawable after 6 months.",
 "D2 Loans": "A member can borrow up to three times their savings. Loan repayment period is at most 24 months.",
 "D3 Interest": "Loan interest is 1 percent per month on the reducing balance, as agreed in the sample policy.",
 "D4 Meetings": "The annual general meeting is held in March. Members receive notice 14 days before.",
 "D5 Penalties": "A late loan instalment attracts a penalty of 5 percent of the instalment amount.",
 "D6 Membership": "New members pay a joining fee of Ksh 1,000 and need two existing members as referees.",
}
vec = TfidfVectorizer(stop_words="english"); M = vec.fit_transform(docs.values())
def retrieve(q, k=2):
    s = (M @ vec.transform([q]).T).toarray().ravel()           # cosine-like score per document
    best = np.argsort(-s)[:k]
    return [(list(docs)[i], round(float(s[i]), 2)) for i in best]
print(retrieve("What is the penalty for paying a loan late?"))
print(retrieve("How much can I borrow?"))

[('D5 Penalties', 0.55), ('D3 Interest', 0.12)]
[('D2 Loans', 0.36), ('D1 Savings', 0.0)]
q = "What is the penalty for paying a loan late?"
hits = [name for name, _ in retrieve(q, 2)]
context = "\n".join(f"[{h}] {docs[h]}" for h in hits)
prompt = (f"Answer using ONLY the context. Cite the [id]. If the answer is not there, say you do not know.\n\n"
          f"Context:\n{context}\n\nQuestion: {q}")
print(prompt)

Answer using ONLY the context. Cite the [id]. If the answer is not there, say you do not know.

Context:
[D5 Penalties] A late loan instalment attracts a penalty of 5 percent of the instalment amount.
[D3 Interest] Loan interest is 1 percent per month on the reducing balance, as agreed in the sample policy.

Question: What is the penalty for paying a loan late?

The final step sends this prompt to an LLM (see 16.9). Quality depends on retrieval: if the right passage is not found, the model cannot use it. Always test retrieval on real questions before blaming the model.

Using it in the real world. Data you need: clean, current documents, split into small passages with an owner who keeps them updated. Validate: a list of real questions with the correct source passage; measure how often it is retrieved. How it fails: outdated or conflicting documents, wrong passage retrieved, a model that ignores the context. Monitor: log questions with no good match and review user complaints weekly. Example: a SACCO help desk answering member questions from its own rulebook.

#16.8 Evaluating LLM outputs

There is no single accuracy score for a chat answer, so evaluate in layers: automatic checks for what is checkable (format, required facts, numbers that appear in the source), then human review of a sample, then tracking over time. Build a small test set of questions with known answers BEFORE choosing a prompt or model.

import re
tests = [("What is the late penalty?", "5 percent", "Late instalments carry a 5 percent penalty [D5 Penalties]."),
         ("What is the joining fee?", "Ksh 1,000", "The joining fee is Ksh 1,500 [D6 Membership]."),
         ("When is the AGM?", "March", "I do not know.")]
def check(expected, answer):
    return {"has_fact": expected.lower() in answer.lower(),
            "cites_source": bool(re.search(r"\[D\d", answer)),
            "says_unknown": "do not know" in answer.lower()}
for q, exp, ans in tests: print(q, "->", check(exp, ans))
passed = sum(check(e, a)["has_fact"] for _, e, a in tests)
print(f"facts correct: {passed} of {len(tests)}")

What is the late penalty? -> {'has_fact': True, 'cites_source': True, 'says_unknown': False}
What is the joining fee? -> {'has_fact': False, 'cites_source': True, 'says_unknown': False}
When is the AGM? -> {'has_fact': False, 'cites_source': False, 'says_unknown': True}
facts correct: 1 of 3

The second answer sounds confident and cites a source, yet the fee is wrong. Style checks alone would pass it; only the fact check catches it. The third refuses honestly, which is safer than guessing, but still counts as a miss on a question the documents CAN answer, so retrieval needs a look.

#16.9 Hallucinations, bias, privacy and safe use

A hallucination is fluent output that is false: an invented statistic, a function that does not exist, a citation to a paper nobody wrote. It happens because the model produces likely text, not checked facts.

  • Hallucination: verify every number, name, date and library function against a trusted source.
  • Bias: models reflect patterns in their training text, so outputs can stereotype. Never use raw LLM output to decide on people (credit, hiring) without fairness checks, see Part 18.
  • Privacy: anything you paste may be stored or seen by others. NEVER paste confidential data: customer names, phone numbers, ID numbers, account details, passwords, API keys, or unpublished company figures. Use synthetic or anonymised samples, or an approved internal tool.
  • Prompt injection: text inside a document or web page can contain hidden instructions. Treat retrieved text as data, not as commands.
  • Over-trust: fluent does not mean correct. A human stays accountable for the result.
import re
def redact(text):
    text = re.sub(r"\b(?:\+?254|0)7\d{8}\b", "[PHONE]", text)       # Kenyan mobile numbers
    text = re.sub(r"\b\d{7,8}\b", "[ID_NUMBER]", text)               # 7-8 digit ID-like numbers
    text = re.sub(r"[\w.]+@[\w.]+\.\w+", "[EMAIL]", text)
    return text
raw = "Customer Jane, phone 0712345678, ID 12345678, email jane@example.com missed her payment."
print(redact(raw))

Customer Jane, phone [PHONE], ID [ID_NUMBER], email [EMAIL] missed her payment.

Careful. Redaction like this is a safety net, not a guarantee: names and free text can still leak. The safest habit is to share only synthetic or aggregated data.

#16.10 Calling an LLM from Python and an AI-assisted workflow

The code below needs the anthropic package, an internet connection and an API key, so it is shown but not run. The key is read from an environment variable; never type it into a notebook or commit it to Git.

import os
from anthropic import Anthropic            # pip install anthropic

client = Anthropic(api_key=os.environ["ANTHROPIC_API_KEY"])
reply = client.messages.create(
    model="claude-sonnet-5-5",
    max_tokens=500,
    temperature=0.2,                        # low: we want consistent answers
    system="You help a data analyst. Say so when you are unsure.",
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": prompt}],     # the RAG prompt built in 16.7
)
print(reply.content[0].text)

Step You do The assistant helps with
1. Frame State the business question Sharpen it, list checks
2. Draft Give schema and synthetic sample rows Draft pandas or SQL code
3. Verify Run it; recompute a second way Suggest more tests
4. Explain Decide what to recommend Tighten wording

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
rng = np.random.default_rng(16)
loans = pd.DataFrame({"product": rng.choice(["Digital", "Salary", "SME"], 400),
                      "defaulted": rng.random(400) < 0.12})                  # synthetic

  • Are row counts sensible after every join? (validate= in merges)
  • Do totals reconcile with the source or an SQL query?

  • Does the wording claim more than the data shows?

#16.11 Attention and the transformer (Advanced)

Older text models read word by word and forgot the start of long sentences. The transformer (the design behind modern LLMs) lets every token look at every other token at once using attention. For each token the model builds three vectors: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I contain?) and a value (what I pass on). The match score of a query with each key says how much to read from each value.

In formula words: weights = softmax(Q times K-transposed divided by sqrt(d)); output = weights times V. The division by sqrt(d), the vector length, keeps scores from growing too large.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
toks = ["the", "rider", "carried", "parcel"]; d = 4
X = rng.normal(size=(4, d))                                  # token embeddings (random here)
Wq, Wk, Wv = (rng.normal(size=(d, d)) for _ in range(3))     # learned in a real model
Q, K, V = X @ Wq, X @ Wk, X @ Wv
scores = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(d)
w = np.exp(scores - scores.max(1, keepdims=True)); w = w / w.sum(1, keepdims=True)   # softmax
out = w @ V
print("attention weights (rows = who looks, columns = who is looked at):")
print(np.round(w, 2))
print("each row sums to 1:", np.allclose(w.sum(1), 1), "| output shape:", out.shape)

attention weights (rows = who looks, columns = who is looked at):
[[0.03 0.01 0.31 0.65]
 [0.   0.   0.13 0.87]
 [0.07 0.08 0.24 0.61]
 [0.14 0.07 0.42 0.37]]
each row sums to 1: True | output shape: (4, 4)

In a real model the weights Wq, Wk, Wv are learned, so 'carried' would attend strongly to 'rider' and 'parcel'. Here they are random, so the pattern means nothing; the mechanics are the point.

ax.text(5, 2.6, "The middle four boxes form one transformer block, stacked many times", ha="center", color=DARK)
ax.text(5, 0.5, "Each block lets every token read from every other token, then refines it", ha="center", color=DARK)
plt.title("A transformer in one picture"); plt.tight_layout()

Tokens enter at the left; many stacked blocks refine them; the last step scores each possible next token.

Position embeddings tell the model word order (attention alone is order-blind). Multi-head attention runs several attention patterns in parallel. Because every token can be processed at once, transformers train fast on GPUs, which is why they scale to billions of parameters.

#16.12 Other generative models and choosing LLM or classical ML (Advanced)

Model Idea in one line Typical output
Diffusion Learn to remove noise step by step; start from pure noise and clean it Images, audio
GAN A generator tries to fool a discriminator that spots fakes Images, synthetic data
VAE Compress data to a small code, then decode new samples from it Images, smooth variations
Autoregressive (LLM) Predict the next token, repeat Text, code

All of them learn a distribution of the training data and sample from it. Diffusion models now dominate image generation; many pipelines combine an LLM with an image model.

#LLM or classical ML?

Task Better first choice Why
Predict loan default from a table Classical ML (Parts 11, 12) Tabular data, need explainable metrics
Forecast monthly sales Classical ML / statistics Numeric series, cheap, testable
Summarise 50 customer complaints LLM Free text, language task
Extract fields from messy messages LLM with validation Flexible wording, check output
Answer questions from a rulebook LLM with RAG Needs your documents, citations
Segment customers by behaviour Classical clustering (Part 13) Numeric features, auditable
Decide who gets credit Classical ML + human policy Fairness and explainability required

Rule of thumb: if the data is a clean table and you can measure the right answer, start with classical ML. If the task is about language and no single right answer exists, an LLM helps, with checks.

Key takeaways
  • Discriminative models predict labels; generative models produce new text, images or data.
  • An LLM predicts the next token; sampling settings (temperature, top-k, top-p) control variety.
  • Embeddings turn words into vectors; cosine similarity measures closeness in meaning.
  • Attention lets each token read from the others; transformers stack this many times.
  • RAG grounds answers in your own documents; test retrieval and check every answer.
  • Never paste confidential data into a tool you do not control; verify every number and claim.
